Proposal for Lumen’s third visual pass · 26 October 2026

The plate and its legend

The engine made every moving thing in Lumen true. The frame around the plate hasn’t caught up. It is still a tabbed settings column from before the rebuild, the figure has four separate keys, and the readouts sit a tab away from what they describe. This pass would turn that column into the figure’s legend and give the plate a trace of what just happened. It would also fix seven animation faults that a frame-by-frame probe measured, and tidy the map. The layouts below are drawn at laptop size around today’s real plate. One section runs the real engine.

Where it stands every specimen, both themes, desktop and phone

The plate is the best thing on the screen. The phycobilisome fanned under photosystem II, the crista bent by its ATP synthase dimers and the purple membrane’s lattice are drawings no textbook animates, and the threads from the stroma’s molecules to the map’s enzymes make the coupling literal. Everything around the plate is older. Codex read the same screenshots and code independently and found the same problems, plus a few I had missed. Its points are marked below.

Unexplained numbers“Glycogen 827” reads as a name with a suffix. Counts, concentrations and fluxes share one style. (Codex)

What already works

Two registers under one camera; the specimens; line weight and trains bound to flux; the stroma’s molecules and their threads; the red spent only on protons; callouts, scale bar, locator; the live caption; opening on a living frame. None of it goes away.

How this was read

All six specimens at 1440 by 900 in both themes and at 390 px, beside Worldline, Miasma and Shoals. Then an in-page probe that tracked every particle and molecule frame by frame for eight seconds per specimen, and Codex’s independent read of the same screenshots and code (gaslamp, thread lumen-layout-read).

Three layouts around the same plate

The site leaves each sim’s control layout to its medium. Lumen’s medium is a scientific plate, and a plate comes with a legend. All three layouts drop the tabs, give the plate one key, move the line switches into it, and add a trace of the last two minutes with your actions marked on it. They differ in where the legend sits. The plates are today’s, so the in-plate changes (next sections) don’t show yet.

A · The legend

The column becomes the figure’s legend, read top to bottom with no tabs. It holds the specimens as their six drawings in one row, the conditions as a sentence whose values you drag, the card of the enzyme you last touched, and Fig. 2, the last two minutes. The lines move into the plate’s key, where they are drawn.

Plate 1120 × 856, up from 1140 × 806 (the caption shrinks to one line). Cost medium. Risk a sentence is an unfamiliar control, and Fig. 2 is small at 320 px.

B · The aligned margin

The column is cut where the plate is cut, and its rules continue the plate’s. Beside panel a are the membrane’s conditions and Δp. Beside b are the network’s knockouts, demand and feeding, which give way to the enzyme card when you touch one. Beside c is the trace’s controls. Panel c is a third register in the plate. (Codex’s second choice.)

Plate 1120 × 716, plus c. Cost medium-high: each specimen cuts the plate at its own height. Risk rows of fixed height overflow, and the margin grows back.

C · The folio

The page scrolls. The conditions are one ruled row in the colophon’s band, the plate runs the full width, and the enzyme strip sits under it. Panel c and the long legend are below the fold. The specimen chooser opens a gallery. (Codex’s first choice.)

Plate 1440 × 780, 29% wider. Cost high: page scroll against canvas pan, and a page model none of the siblings use. Risk what you changed and what it did are on different screens.

My pick is A, with one idea from B: the legend’s rules can line up with the plate’s, so the column reads as part of the figure. Codex picked C because it gives the plate the most room and the bench a natural place. I’d keep C for phones, where it fits best (below), but on a laptop it puts what you changed and what it did on different screens. A keeps both in view, and it is how a figure in a paper is read: the plate, then its legend.

This is A’s column at full size, driven by Lumen’s engine itself, bundled into this page (the cyanobacterium, warmed for 40 model seconds). The enzyme

Fig. 2 The last two minutes of wall time, with what you did

The sentence. The conditions are what the caption already says, so in A they are the legend’s first sentence, and each value in it is the control. A specimen without an uncoupler or oxygen leaves that clause out. The keyboard path is a slider role on each value, and phones drag the same way.

The card. It replaces the inspector’s dropdown and follows your last click on the plate. A dropdown stays for keyboard use. The rate is drawn as a budget, capacity times each factor on a log scale, so you see which factor holds an enzyme back. After a hold, the card shows the flux control coefficients.

Fig. 2. Today’s sparklines, grown into the bench’s first register. Its time is wall time, two minutes, because at 30× that is four seconds of the cell’s. That’s enough to watch a pulse spread and fade. It isn’t enough for an experiment, which is the next section’s point.

The ladder (the Z-scheme from Stats) becomes Fig. 2’s other view, one word away. Δψ, ΔpH, the pools’ shares, stress and ATP’s sources go into the plate’s key, where the counts are already.

What Fig. 2 grows into milestone 2, the bench

The engine already reproduces the classic oxygen-electrode experiment. In the animal cell (Chance and Williams 1955), extra ADP demand speeds respiration about 3.6-fold, and oligomycin cuts it back. An uncoupler then releases it to its maximum, and cyanide stops it. Below is Lumen’s own run, 230 model seconds.

One thing this shows for the layout: at the default 30× slowdown this run takes two hours. The bench needs clocks faster than life, 1× and beyond, with the plate thinned to match. The window already supports that, since a centre past eight turnovers a second is drawn one turnover in w. So Fig. 2 is wall time now, and its axis switches to model time when the bench lands. The layout doesn’t change.

Phones the folio, in any case

On a phone the page scrolls through the figure in reading order, and each part of the legend follows the register it describes. That means splitting today’s single canvas into one per register. One-finger vertical drags scroll the page, sideways drags pan panel a, and pinching zooms.

Order of work

  1. The animations, all seven, with the probe kept as a regression check. These don’t depend on the layout, so they can start now.
  2. Inside the plate: one key, the fitted map, junction placement, counts, threads, pulse marks, the colophon’s band, a one-line caption.
  3. The legend (or the layout you choose): the specimen row, the sentence, the enzyme card, Fig. 2 and its ladder view; the tabs and the old Stats go.
  4. Phones: one canvas per register, the folio.
  5. Verify and land: every specimen in both themes at 1440, 1024 and 390, reduced motion, 60 fps, the probe and the engine tests; Codex reviews the diff; AGENTS.md and about.md updated; committed in projects/lumen, with the pointer bumped in the parent.

For you to decide

1. The layout

A (the legend), B (the aligned margin) or C (the folio), or a mix? I’d build A on desktop and C on phones.

2. The sentence

Conditions as a sentence of draggable values, or as labelled dials in the same order and place? The demo above has the sentence; dials are the safer, more familiar choice.

3. Fig. 2 now

Add the trace of the last two minutes now, as the bench’s first register, or wait for milestone 2? I’d add it now, since it is what makes a pulse readable.

4. The enzyme card

Follow your last click in the legend, as in the demo? Or pin the card on the plate next to the enzyme? Pinned is closer to the thing, but it covers the plate.

5. The carriers

Persistent PQ, PC and Fd that travel, load and ride (the teleport fix) mean a real rewrite of how electrons are drawn. Is that in scope for this pass?

6. Anything I missed

Which animations looked buggy to you? I’d like to match the probe against what you saw, and add anything it can’t count.
