# Lumen — Interactive Cellular Metabolism Simulator

Lumen is a browser-based model of a cell's energy metabolism. Each organism preset is a kinetic model: its metabolites and cofactors are concentrations, its reactions run at rates set by thermodynamics, saturation and regulation, and an implicit integrator advances them in model time. The figure is a window onto that model. Above, the cell's membrane is drawn in cross-section with the cofactor pools under it; below, its carbon network is drawn as a transit map. Its clock runs from 300× slower than life to 1,000× faster (30× slower by default), and the legend states which.

## The Model

Every chemical is named by its BiGG identifier, and its formula and charge come from BiGG's *E. coli* model iJO1366. Each reaction is one enzyme's reaction, balanced in elements and charge, so opposing enzymes such as hexokinase and glucose-6-phosphatase, or phosphofructokinase and fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase, are separate reactions. β-oxidation is resolved by chain length from C16 to C4. Fatty-acid synthesis runs on its own enzymes. Conserved groups (adenine nucleotides, NAD, NADP, coenzyme A, the quinone pool and the protein carriers) are checked by tests: the network may hold no conservation law that was not specified.

Standard reaction free energies come from eQuilibrator (pH 7, ionic strength 0.25 M, pMg 3). Membrane reactions take theirs from the midpoint potentials of their carriers, and the photosystems from the energy of the photons they absorb. Every cycle's free energies sum to zero.

Each rate is the product of a capacity, a regulation factor, a saturation factor (convenience kinetics) and a thermodynamic factor, 1 − e^(ΔG/RT) (the separable form of Noor et al. 2013). A reaction therefore stops at equilibrium and never runs against its ΔG; more enzyme makes it faster but cannot move its equilibrium. Regulation is smooth: ATP, citrate, NADH/NAD⁺ and other effectors slow or speed their enzymes, and light switches the Calvin cycle's enzymes on through thioredoxin and the enzymes that would undo its work off; those stay off until the light is nearly gone, so at dusk the Calvin cycle closes before the night's pathways open. ATP demand, growth and the chloroplast's export of sugar to its cell are dials. Glycogen and starch are polymers, so how much is stored changes their enzymes' saturation but not their equilibria: synthesis needs 3-PGA, the Calvin cycle's product, so the store takes what the cycle makes beyond its needs, and phosphorylase opens as AMP rises, so a cell draws on its store as it needs to. Growth draws on the precursors of an *E. coli* core biomass composition, with its nitrogen as glutamate and glutamine and its lipid as palmitoyl-CoA, and runs at the pace of the scarcest. Ammonium enters through glutamine synthetase and glutamate synthase (glutamate dehydrogenase in the animal cell), so assimilating it spends α-ketoglutarate (growth's transaminations give most of it back), and glutamine synthetase follows the balance of α-ketoglutarate and glutamine, the cell's carbon against its nitrogen.

The system is stiff, so it is integrated by a linearly implicit Rosenbrock method (ROS2) with a sparse Jacobian. Its steps keep every conservation law exactly. A trajectory depends only on model time, not on how frames slice it, and the tests check this.

## Membrane Energetics

The proton-motive force has two parts, each with its own state: Δψ from the net charge moved across the membrane, and ΔpH from the protons the lumen side has gained, mostly bound to buffers. A pumped proton costs F·Δp in a reaction's free energy, so the gradient pushes back on its pumps without a separate rule. ANT's exchange of ATP⁴⁻ for ADP³⁻ spends only Δψ, and the pyruvate and phosphate carriers, each symporting a proton, only ΔpH. Counter-ion channels and K⁺/H⁺ exchange let one part relax into the other: mitochondria and bacteria keep most of Δp as Δψ, thylakoids as ΔpH (the lumen near pH 5.5 in the light). Raising the exchange collapses ΔpH as nigericin does; raising the channels collapses Δψ as valinomycin does.

β-oxidation's electrons enter the chain through ETF, the electron-transferring flavoprotein, and ETF:quinone oxidoreductase, so fatty acids feed the quinone pool after complex I. In the cyanobacterium, NDH-1 takes ferredoxin rather than NADH (Schuller et al. 2019): in the light it returns PSI's electrons to plastoquinone, pumping as it does (cyclic flow), and in the dark it takes them from NADPH through ferredoxin–NADP⁺ reductase run backward, so the cell respires on its stored glycogen.

ATP synthase takes one proton per subunit of its c-ring and makes three ATP a turn, so its cost per ATP is the ring's size over three: c₈ in mammalian mitochondria (Watt et al. 2010), c₁₄ in chloroplasts (Seelert et al. 2000) and in *Synechocystis* (Pogoryelov et al. 2007), c₁₀ in *E. coli* (Jiang et al. 2001), which stands in for the anaerobe. Fig. 2's ladder view derives the yield from the cell's own reactions: a P/O ratio of 30/11, about 2.7, from NADH in the animal cell, and 1.3 ATP per NADPH from linear flow in a thylakoid, short of the Calvin cycle's 1.5. The halophile's A-type synthase is drawn with its two peripheral stalks. With little ADP the gradient rises until the respiratory chain nearly stalls, and NADH goes unburned; adding ATP demand lets ATP synthase drain the gradient and the chain runs faster. This is respiratory control, and it emerges from the thermodynamics. ATP synthase runs whichever way its ΔG says: forward when its protons' worth of Δp exceeds the free energy of making ATP, backward otherwise, splitting ATP to pump protons as the fermenting anaerobe does. IF1 holds the mitochondrial synthase against running backward when Δp collapses. AtpΘ binds the cyanobacterium's as it stalls and stops it splitting ATP, so in anoxia the cell keeps its ATP and lets Δp go; the chloroplast's is switched off in the dark and held the same way, so with no respiration of its own its thylakoid goes flat at night. Uncoupling protein adds a conductance that collapses Δp and speeds respiration.

Linear photosynthetic flow runs from water at PSII through plastoquinone, cytochrome b₆f and plastocyanin to PSI, ferredoxin and FNR, which reduces NADP⁺. Cyclic flow returns PSI's electrons to plastoquinone. Where the Calvin cycle cannot take the electrons, they go to oxygen (the water–water cycle). Bacteriorhodopsin pumps a proton per photon.

## Organisms

Five presets each put their own organism and membrane under the microscope:

- **Cyanobacterium:** a thylakoid with phycobilisomes, where respiration and photosynthesis share plastoquinone, cytochrome b₆f and plastocyanin. It fixes CO₂, stores glycogen and makes fatty acids. By day growth takes most of the carbon it fixes, about two-thirds over a day, and the store most of the rest; growth runs only in the light, so at night it the cell respires its glycogen through the oxidative pentose phosphate pathway, whose NADPH reaches NDH-1 through ferredoxin, at a few percent of its daytime photosynthesis, and lasts the night. Without oxygen it ferments to lactate.
- **Animal cell:** a mitochondrial crista. Glycolysis runs in the cytosol, and the Krebs cycle, β-oxidation and the respiratory chain run in the mitochondrial matrix, joined by lumped carriers (pyruvate, ADP/ATP, phosphate, the malate–aspartate shuttle). Without oxygen it ferments to lactate.
- **Obligate anaerobe:** a plasma membrane under a peptidoglycan wall, with no chain at all. It ferments to ethanol, and its ATP synthase runs backward to keep the membrane energized.
- **Plant chloroplast:** a thylakoid with photosystem II in a stacked granum and photosystem I and ATP synthase in the stroma lamella. Its medium is the plant cell's cytosol, which supplies ATP at night and takes triose phosphate, which it makes into sucrose. By day the chloroplast exports triose and stores starch; at night RuBisCO is off and it exports from its starch, as triose standing in for the maltose and glucose a leaf's chloroplast exports. RuBisCO activase needs ATP, so in dim light the Calvin cycle slows before it drains the stroma's ATP.
- **Archaeon:** a halophile's purple membrane, where bacteriorhodopsin alone powers ATP synthesis in the light.

Custom shows a composite membrane with all fourteen complexes on a composite cell. Switching a line off knocks out its enzymes; a line the organism lacks is locked, with the reason.

## The Window

Panel a shows a fixed slab of the cell: the drawn length of membrane, 16.5 nm of the space under it, 20 nm deep. The cofactor molecules drawn there are the slab's mean counts at the cell's concentrations, so the animal cell's matrix holds a few free NADH among many NAD⁺. FAD stays bound to its enzymes and is not drawn. The lumen's protons are drawn one per 2 mV of Δp, not counted, because almost all translocated protons are bound to buffers.

Each complex drawn is a representative centre turning over at its turnover number times the share of its capacity in use, slowed. Its turnovers are what moves: an electron (standing for a pair) entering at NDH-1, SDH or PSII waits on its carrier at each later complex until that complex turns over, which pumps that complex's protons, uses its oxygen or makes its NADPH. ATP synthase's rotor turns a third of a turn per ATP and its head shows the binding change. Above eight turnovers a second a centre is drawn one turnover in several, and the caption says so. A complex that makes or spends a cofactor (ATP synthase, FNR, NDH-1, NNT) draws a molecule in, changes it and lets it go back out into the pool, which stays well mixed. When an enzyme on the map makes or spends a drawn cofactor, a dotted thread runs from the molecule it changed to the enzyme. Superoxide radicals appear where the chain leaks them and are cleared at the model's own rate. At 2.5× zoom each complex shows its cofactors, the sites electrons step between.

## The Verb

Clicking an enzyme on the map or a complex in the membrane pulses it: its activity rises fivefold and fades over about 1.5 s of model time. Right-clicking inhibits it the same way, and pulses stack. Watching where a push spreads and where it dies shows how control is shared. Holding an enzyme instead raises its activity by 10% and keeps it there. Lumen then runs two copies of the cell from that moment, one held and one not, until both reach a steady state. Each flux's response gives the enzyme's flux control coefficient over it (Kacser & Burns 1973): the fractional change in the flux per fractional change in the enzyme. Coefficients are reported only once a steady state exists, for the enzyme's own flux and for the cell's glucose uptake, carbon fixation, water splitting, oxygen use, ATP synthesis and growth.

## The Map

The carbon network is drawn as a transit map: glycolysis runs the corridor, the Calvin cycle runs back along it, the pentose phosphate pathway loops above it and closes through PGI (carrying the F6P that goes round the oxidative branch again), the Krebs cycle is a ring line and β-oxidation a loop. Line weight and train speed read each segment's carbon flux in mM of carbon per second, and the chevron trains point the way carbon runs. Each enzyme carries the cofactors it makes (+) or spends (−) as glyphs after its name. An enzyme held back by a regulator, or by the dark, shows the effector across the track: ⊣ inhibits, → is needed, dashed while it only slows the enzyme.

## Reactive Oxygen and Damage

Complex I and the Q-cycle leak superoxide, faster as Δp rises. Superoxide dismutase, catalase and glutathione peroxidase (which spends NADPH) remove it and its peroxide. What remains damages the membrane: the damage raises the proton leak and inactivates aconitase, and the membrane's lipids are drawn peroxidised in proportion. Repair spends ATP, so a cell without ATP stays damaged.

## Educational Use

The figure's legend, beside it, is where you set the conditions and read the results. Its conditions list the light and oxygen (as fractions of full sun and of air), ATP demand and growth, and the clock, and each value is a control: drag it, step it with the arrow keys, or click it for a dial. Below that sits a card for the enzyme you last clicked, which shows its rate as a budget: its capacity times the thermodynamic, saturation and regulation factors (and light, a switch or a pulse where they act), so the factor holding it back is the one that cuts the bar most. Holding an enzyme there or on the plate compares the cell with an unchanged copy and reports the enzyme's flux control coefficients. Fig. 2 traces the last two minutes of watching (its axis gives the cell's time they held) of Δp, the ATP share of the adenylates, the reduced share of the cell's main carrier and its headline flux, with an arrow at each change you made. Its other view is a live Z-scheme: each carrier at its midpoint potential, with the electrons in flight drawn on the carriers they have reached. The lines are knocked out from the plate's key, which also counts the drawn molecules. The canonical behaviours of bioenergetics follow from the model rather than from rules: respiratory control, the Pasteur effect (glycolysis speeds up when oxygen is removed), the effects of uncoupling, and the switch between light and dark metabolism.

## Accessibility

Lumen provides keyboard shortcuts, labeled controls, and light, dark and sunlight-following themes. The enzyme card's select and buttons reach every enzyme without a pointer, each condition's value is a slider to the keyboard and screen readers, and the knockouts are checkboxes as well as the key's rows. Line states carry patterns as well as color: switched-off lines are dashed, gates are dashed or barred, and trains are chevrons. With reduced motion requested, Lumen opens on a still frame and moves only after the first interaction.

## Limitations

Most kinetic parameters and many pool sizes are illustrative, not measured. Counter-ions are lumped into one channel and one K⁺/H⁺ exchanger with K⁺ held constant, so how Δp divides is set by two conductances rather than by ion concentrations. The halophile's A-type ring is uncounted, so its twelve subunits are illustrative. The animal cell makes no fatty acid (that needs citrate carried out of the matrix), so it grows only while its medium has fatty acid: about a quarter of its growth dial, for some 15 minutes from the default medium, or again once fed. The halophile has no amino-acid metabolism, and the biomass composition is *E. coli*'s in every organism. The cyanobacterium has no carbon-concentrating mechanism, and below about a fifth of full sun its growth stops and its store takes its carbon: the regulation that shares carbon between them is a stand-in. Near 40% of full sun its energy charge cycles every couple of minutes.
